01 • Fiscal & Real Estate Returns

Walkability Is Economically Beneficial to Everyone

Before I built a wall I’d ask to know
What I was walling in or walling out,
And to whom I was like to give offense.

— Robert Frost, Mending Wall

Suburban residents value cul-de-sacs because they eliminate fast cut-through car traffic, keeping residential streets quiet and safe for children. Traditional subdivision design, however, throws the baby out with the bathwater by blocking pedestrians and cyclists too. Installing a paved 8-to-10-foot connector path with entrance bollards achieves filtered permeability—preserving 100% of the traffic-calming benefits of a dead-end street for cars while restoring full grid connectivity on foot, stroller, or bicycle.

Fears that opening a connector will flood quiet streets with strangers ignore basic pedestrian physics. Unlike car trips, human walking trips follow a steep distance-decay curve: roughly 85% to 95% of routine foot traffic on a suburban connector comes from people living within a 0.25-to-0.5-mile radius (a 5-to-10-minute walk). Nobody drives across town to park and walk down a neighborhood path; the “outsiders” using an inter-neighborhood link are simply the families, dog walkers, and commuters who already live a few blocks away.

Stakeholder Economic Mechanism Documented Financial Impact
Homeowners & Multifamily Destination walkability to schools, parks, retail, and transit expands buyer and renter demand across demographics. +3% to +15% home price premium within 0.25 to 0.5 miles; faster leasing and stronger downturn resilience.
Municipalities Short connectors remove 800-yard errands from congested arterials and reduce structural axle wear (fourth-power law). $15,000–$45,000 per path link vs. $2M–$8M to widen a single suburban intersection.
School Districts Safe off-street connectors allow students living within 0.5 miles to walk rather than ride mandated “hazard buses”. A $30,000 path letting 50 students walk saves $50,000–$90,000/yr (6-month payback).
HOA Boards Dedicating a 10-foot public pedestrian easement retires depreciating perimeter fences from reserve studies. Eliminates $15,000–$60,000+ in fence replacement costs every 12 to 20 years and shifts maintenance to the city.

Connective pedestrian paths deliver some of the highest returns on investment in local infrastructure because they leverage land that already exists rather than requiring billions in new highway right-of-way. Roughly 20% to 25% of all U.S. car trips are under 1 mile, and nearly half are under 3 miles. In severed cul-de-sac suburbs, those short errands clog collector intersections and impose heavy structural asphalt wear—under civil engineering’s fourth-power law, a single SUV inflicts hundreds of thousands of times more structural stress on pavement than a person walking or cycling.

In residential real estate, destination walkability is a permanent locational moat that no interior renovation can replicate. Car-only subdivisions exclude entire segments of modern buyers, whereas connected neighborhoods appeal simultaneously to parents tired of acting as full-time chauffeurs, remote workers seeking midday walkability, and older adults aging in place who want independence after they stop driving. When evaluated strictly through balance sheets rather than habit, walling off neighborhoods is an expensive luxury. A few hundred feet of concrete or compacted gravel converts isolated parcels into higher-yielding assets, trims municipal and school operating budgets, and keeps thousands of dollars per household circulating in the local economy instead of vanishing into vehicle depreciation and fuel.

02 • The Selective Exclusion Paradox

Good Fences Keep Out Good Neighbors—and Give Bad Ones a Place to Hide

It is their nature to be dark
Themselves, keeping a kind
Of thieves’ kitchen for the things
Sequestered from the world

— Howard Nemerov, Pockets

To be clear, we are not talking about a small backyard fence to keep dogs and toddlers safe, or screening out Bill next door who piles old lawnmowers along the property line, or blocking Nebby Nancy from calling the HOA over a patch of crabgrass she can see from her patio. Around immediate residential curtilage, privacy fencing makes complete sense. Perimeter fencing along larger parcels, ravines, and neighborhood boundaries is an entirely different phenomenon—and it counterintuitively breeds the exact disorder it is meant to prevent.

The Selective Exclusion Paradox A standard chain-link, wood, or wrought-iron perimeter fence relies on social compliance rather than impenetrable security. It blocks rule-following guardians—dog walkers, joggers, and parents with strollers—while failing to stop anyone determined to walk around the end, slip through brush, or hop over. Once someone is behind the fence, the barrier stops protecting the property and starts shielding disorder from view.

When an apartment complex or subdivision erects a perimeter fence along a wooded edge or topographical drop like a dry streambed, it manufactures a blind edge—land that legally belongs to the owner but is functionally abandoned in daily operations. Unhoused individuals seeking shelter and people looking to dump trash, tires, or appliances illegally actively choose sites with two attributes: visual concealment and zero predictable foot traffic. A fenced-off ravine or far wooded corner offers both. Once a few bags of trash or discarded clothes sit unseen for a month, visible neglect signals abandonment, locking in a self-reinforcing cycle of dumping and decay.

Removing a section of perimeter fence and replacing it with a simple, open walking path reverses that dynamic immediately:

  • Eliminating Concealment Through Directional Flow: Connecting paths turn stagnant dead zones into active thoroughfares. Neighbors walking between complexes, subdivisions, or nearby shops move straight through former dark edges, removing the quiet, undisturbed cover required for dumping, vandalism, or encampments.
  • An Unpaid Daily Inspection Crew: Security does not require constant policing—only unpredictable legitimate presence. Regular dog walkers and runners are consistently the first to spot clogged storm culverts before a flash flood, fallen limbs threatening power lines, broken glass, or brush-fire hazards, allowing property managers to fix a $100 issue before it becomes a $10,000 cleanup. Properties with active perimeter trails likewise see fewer car break-ins and catalytic converter thefts than complexes surrounded by unmonitored blind edges.
03 • Suburban Psychology & Safety

Connectivity Is Not a Security Risk

Everyone I knew was living
The same desolate luxury,
Each ashamed of the same things:
Innocence and privacy.

— Tracy K. Smith, Garden of Eden

Pearl-clutching suburbanites everywhere fear the person on foot. Because the physical layout of suburbia is exclusionary by design, anyone passing through without a car registers as unfamiliar and therefore suspect.

Suburbia is built on fragile psychological ground. Most Americans hold the bulk of their net worth in residential real estate, conditioning homeowners to view their neighborhood not as a shared civic commons, but as a vulnerable financial asset whose value depends on exclusivity. When social status is tied to living behind a buffer zone, opening a direct walkway to a neighboring subdivision or apartment complex feels like a personal demotion. Manicured suburban enclaves cultivate an aesthetic of sterile, predictable control; in that environment, an unvetted pedestrian crossing a property line triggers a visceral contagion disgust response—a fear that outside disorder or lower social status will bleed across the perimeter and taint the enclave’s purity.

Social psychology’s Contact Hypothesis demonstrates that routine, low-stakes exposure to diverse neighbors reduces prejudice and lowers threat perception. Sealed subdivisions engineer the exact opposite condition. By eliminating casual daily encounters on sidewalks, enclosed neighborhoods atrophy residents’ social resilience. In a connected neighborhood, seeing someone walk past is unremarkable background noise; in a sealed cul-de-sac, any unfamiliar face on foot registers as an acute anomaly, fueling hyper-vigilant Nextdoor posts, Ring camera surveillance loops, and unnecessary calls to police.

The Reality of Suburban Crime and Unlit Paths

Virtually nobody is burglarizing houses and jogging away down a greenway with stolen televisions, despite popular suburban folklore. Suburban property crime relies on motor vehicles and arterial road access to transport goods quickly, and it thrives on enclosed, hidden spaces rather than open walkways. The very attributes that make a walled-off subdivision feel protected paradoxically make it more fragile and vulnerable to disorder than a connected neighborhood.

The geography of opportunistic crime follows two basic rules: go where disorder is unseen and tolerated, and go where targets exist. An unlit path at night may appear at first glance to meet the first condition, but it decisively fails the second. Offenders do not sit in dark woods for hours waiting for a lone walker who never arrives. Multi-mile trails close at dusk and see minimal night traffic, and completely dark stretches actually experience less late-night loitering than dimly lit areas with benches because total darkness is uncomfortable for hanging out. The few people who do use neighborhood paths after dark are ordinary residents walking a dog, jogging with a headlamp, or strolling with a partner—not imaginary highwaymen lying in wait.

Protecting the One Exception: Homes Directly Abutting a Path Entrance

While neighborhood-wide property values consistently rise with trail connectivity, the one legitimate exception involves the one or two homes whose yards directly border a new cut-through. Even when 95% of a neighborhood gains equity from improved walkability, it is unfair to ask the immediately adjacent homeowner to absorb a potential −1% to −5% localized privacy friction penalty on behalf of a public amenity. Fortunately, straightforward upfront design and governance tools eliminate that burden:

  • City- or Developer-Funded Privacy Screening: Project capital budgets should cover installing a new 6-foot opaque privacy fence and a staggered evergreen buffer along abutting lot lines at zero cost to the homeowner, replacing aging chain-link boundaries and blocking eye-level sightlines.
  • Fair-Market Compensation and Tax Abatements: When a municipality acquires a 10-to-15-foot easement strip from an existing lot, the owner should receive fair-market land value plus proximity compensation upfront, paired where available with a multi-year local property tax abatement.
  • Recorded Maintenance and Liability Indemnification: Recording an explicit municipal or HOA maintenance covenant—guaranteeing biweekly mowing, trash removal, and snow clearing—alongside statutory liability immunity ensures the adjacent owner carries zero upkeep or legal exposure.
  • Private Gate Access as a Resale Advantage: Once a connector is built with proper fencing, many abutting owners install a lockable private gate in their back fence. Direct, step-out access to a path network without crossing busy roads routinely turns a border lot into a sought-after premium listing for families, runners, and dog owners.
04 • Chokepoints & Good Neighborliness

Parcels Straddling Pinch Points Hold a Unique Opportunity

This subdivision is supposed to be off limits to me,
who lives outside its gates. Yet, I trespass. I want
to walk off highway, somewhere quiet and plowed,
where my dogs won’t worry cars and I won’t worry for them.

— CMarie Fuhrman, Trespass

A lot sitting on the sole narrow gap between two severed neighborhoods carries that spatial pressure as an inherent physical attribute of the land—no different from buying a house next to an elementary school playground, under an airport flight path, or at the bottom of a drainage slope. Getting furious at neighbors for walking through an open gap is like buying a house in a valley and getting angry at rainwater for flowing downhill. The root cause of the friction is the developer who maximized lot yield by omitting a 10-foot public walkway and the municipality that approved a disconnected street grid. Directing anger at a neighbor walking their dog or a kid walking to school punishes ordinary people for adapting to broken infrastructure.

Under Anglo-American property law, the right to exclude others is a core tenet of private ownership. Unless a public pedestrian easement is recorded on the plat—or decades of continuous, unpermitted use satisfy the strict threshold for a prescriptive easement—a homeowner is legally entitled to object to uninvited passage and erect a fence. However, holding a legal right to exclude does not make outrage reasonable when someone buys a natural geographic bridge and expects human beings to ignore basic geometry.

The Double Standard of Two-Street Through-Driveways

When a homeowner owns a parcel with a driveway spanning two roads, they practice privatized permeability—monopolizing a network shortcut for their own daily convenience while attempting to deny that exact same spatial efficiency to everyone else. By using the driveway to exit onto whichever street faces their destination, the owner proves every single day that going around the block is an unnecessary waste of time.
