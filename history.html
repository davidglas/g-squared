How Car Supremacy Was Engineered

Car supremacy in the United States was not an organic free-market outcome; it was engineered between the 1930s and 1960s through deliberate federal subsidies, lending rules, and municipal zoning laws that starved walkable cities of capital while underwriting low-density sprawl.

1. Federal Mortgage Underwriting and the FHA (1934–1940s)

During the Great Depression and immediately after World War II, the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) and the GI Bill revolutionized homeownership by backing 30-year, low-down-payment mortgages. However, federal underwriting guidelines came with strict spatial strings attached:

  • Subsidizing the Fringe: FHA rules heavily favored newly built, detached single-family homes on undeveloped land over repairs or purchases in older, denser urban neighborhoods, making it cheaper to buy a new suburban house than to rent a city apartment.
  • Mandating Car-Oriented Layouts: FHA land-planning bulletins explicitly discouraged walkable grid streets and mixed-use blocks, requiring developers to use segregated residential pods and cul-de-sacs to qualify for federally insured loans.

2. The 1956 Federal-Aid Highway Act

Passage of the Interstate Highway Act created a massive structural imbalance in transportation funding. The federal government agreed to pay 90% of highway construction costs (with states covering just 10%), funded through a dedicated gas tax in the Highway Trust Fund.

  • Asymmetric Subsidies: While roads received an automatic, self-replenishing river of federal cash, public transit systems were expected to survive entirely on passenger fares while paying property taxes on their tracks and stations.
  • Gutting Urban Cores: Rather than stopping at city limits, interstates were routed directly through dense, walkable neighborhoods—often targeting low-income and Black communities under the banner of "urban renewal"—demolishing hundreds of thousands of homes and severing local street grids with multi-lane concrete trenches.

3. Single-Use Zoning and Parking Minimums

Post-war municipalities aggressively adopted Euclidean zoning, which legally prohibited mixing land uses. Apartments, corner stores, offices, and workshops were banned from single-family residential zones, forcing homes, jobs, and groceries miles apart.

  • The Asphalt Buffer: Cities simultaneously instituted mandatory off-street parking minimums, requiring every restaurant, office, and store to build enough parking spaces to handle peak holiday demand.
  • Destroying Walkability: Parking minimums forced buildings to be pushed back behind massive moats of asphalt. Even when two stores sat next to each other, walking between them meant crossing acres of exposed, unshaded parking lots—turning short errands into car trips.

4. Assembly-Line Tract Housing (The Levittown Model)

Post-war developers like Levitt & Sons applied Fordist assembly-line techniques to homebuilding, buying up cheap agricultural land miles beyond existing streetcar lines and erecting thousands of standardized houses at breakneck speed. Because these subdivisions were built entirely outside existing transit corridors and lacked commercial centers, owning a car—and soon a second car—shifted from a luxury to a basic prerequisite for household survival.

5. The Dismantling of Streetcar Networks

In the 1920s, the United States possessed the most extensive urban streetcar and interurban electric rail network in the world. Over the next three decades, that network collapsed due to a combination of municipal fare caps (many lines were legally locked into 5-cent fares despite post-war inflation), competition from automobiles clogging shared streets, and corporate buyouts. Holding companies such as National City Lines—backed by General Motors, Firestone Tire, and Standard Oil—purchased dozens of private streetcar systems across the country, tore up the tracks, and replaced them with diesel buses, cementing the automobile's monopoly on American streets.

Together, these policies locked in a self-reinforcing loop: once land was spread too thin for walking or transit to function efficiently, driving became mandatory for basic economic participation, and every subsequent road widening or parking mandate further hollowed out the pedestrian realm.
